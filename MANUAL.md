# Realm Builder — Player Manual

Realm Builder is an indirect kingdom sim. You do not command heroes or choose their fights. You raise buildings, shape the land, post rewards, and pay for peace; the people of the realm decide what to do with the opportunities you create.

## The Realm at a Glance

- **Your job:** build a settlement that can feed itself, collect taxes, attract adventurers, and survive the wilds.
- **Your lever:** buildings and incentives. Halls attract certain hero classes, bounties pull heroes toward lairs, and tribute buys time from hostile factions.
- **The map is dangerous:** goblins raid, bandits rob the roads, hags curse farms, and some halls carry their own risks.
- **There is no hard game-over.** A bad raid can raze buildings and kill people, but the realm can rebuild. The Palace's household also earns a slow trickle of gold so a broke realm can recover.

## Controls and the Interface

- **Pan and zoom:** drag the map with one finger or the mouse; pinch to zoom. The map is larger than the screen.
- **Inspect:** tap a building, unit, den, or loot pile to open its card. The card explains what it does, what it is doing now, and the actions available there.
- **Build:** choose a hall in the build bar, tap a map tile to preview it, then press the check button to commit. The ghost turns red when the site is blocked. The X cancels.
- **Minimap:** bottom-right. It shows only charted ground, the Palace, charted lairs, and heroes. Tap it to jump the main view.
- **Log:** bottom-left. Reopens the event scrollback, with tick stamps, so you can catch up on raids, arrivals, curses, and tribute.
- **Hero Roster:** top-left button. Lists every hero, their class, level, and current activity; tap a row to inspect that hero.
- **Dev:** top-right. Adjusts simulation speed and can reveal the whole map for troubleshooting.
- **Manual:** the book button in the bottom-right corner opens this manual at any time.

## The Core Loop

1. Build farms and houses to support people.
2. Let peasants work farms and carry provisions back to the Palace.
3. Tax-bearing buildings fill their purses; the tax collector walks a round and brings the coin home.
4. Spend gold on more halls, terrain work, bounties, tribute, and gear.
5. Halls attract heroes. Heroes explore, hunt, shop, rest, fight, and chase the rewards you post.
6. Use bounties and tribute to point all that autonomy at the problems you care about.
7. Chart the wilds, clear dangerous lairs, and keep the settlement growing.

## Gold, Food, and People

**Gold** is the main limiter. It comes from taxes, markets where heroes spend loot, the Palace's slow household trickle, and gnome trade. It goes into construction, terrain work, bounties, tribute, and the wider economy.

**Taxes** accrue in farms, houses, taverns, and markets. The tax collector visits taxed buildings on a round and carries the proceeds back to the Palace. Uncollected tax waits in the building's purse, so long rounds and unsafe roads matter. Bandits like to ambush laden collectors.

**Farms** define the realm's provisions capacity. Peasants work fields and deliver food to the Palace. A farm with no free farmer, or a cursed field, produces less. **Houses** raise the population ceiling and pay tax.

**Population growth** needs three things: room under the housing cap, enough farm capacity, and a steady flow of delivered provisions. If the population outgrows its food, people begin to leave slowly rather than the realm collapsing all at once. Population never falls below one.

The population number is abstract; only a small visible workforce of peasants walks the map. The rest are represented by the settlement's growth, taxes, and hero cap.

**Markets** are where heroes bank their loot. The hero's purse becomes realm gold, which makes markets a useful way to turn adventuring into income.

## Land, Fog, and Terrain

The map starts mostly uncharted. The Palace charts its surroundings at the start. Heroes reveal a wide area as they roam; workers reveal only a little. Charted ground stays charted.

Fog is a real rule, not just a visual filter:

- You cannot build on unexplored land.
- Lairs, loot piles, and hostile creatures remain hidden until the realm has charted them.
- Heroes do not plan around lairs or treasure they have not heard of.
- The minimap follows the same knowledge.

**Terrain types** matter. Grass is the normal building ground. Forest and marsh are walkable but usually not buildable until improved; the herbalist grove and ranger hut are the exceptions. Water and mountains block movement and construction.

**Draining marsh** and **clearing woods** are ordered works, not instant purchases. Pay the fee, then peasants or a helpful hero invest labour until the tile becomes grass. Terrain work leaves pollution behind, and a druid may eventually turn against a realm that defiles too much wild land.

## Building Rules

- Every building has a footprint. The Palace is large; most halls are one or two tiles across. The placement ghost shows the full footprint.
- Buildings need a one-tile breathing gap from other buildings, including the Palace.
- Every covered tile must be charted, clear of dens and other works, and on legal terrain.
- Construction sites are raised by peasants. A site is not a working building until it is finished.
- Demolition is available from the inspector for friendly buildings. It refunds nothing, and the Palace cannot be demolished.

## Halls and Their People

**Farm** — provides provisions capacity and pays tax. Peasants work it and carry food home.

**House** — raises the population cap, pays tax, and is the source of the realm's people.

**Guardhouse** — trains guards and attracts warriors. Guards patrol near the Palace, intercept raiders, and defend the realm. They do not march off to siege distant lairs; that is hero work.

**Tavern** — pays tax, attracts rogues, and is a place heroes rest and recover. Rogues especially like its rumours and easy company.

**Market** — pays tax and lets heroes spend loot, turning their purses back into realm gold. Wealthy heroes may also shop for what they need.

**Shrine** — attracts paladins and gives praying heroes a place to reflect. Its aura also wards nearby graveyards against hostile risings.

**Blacksmith** — forges gear over time and attracts warriors and dwarf craftsmen. Heroes with coin and a free hand buy gear from the rack; the purchase returns gold to the realm.

**Wizard Tower** — attracts wizards, channels mana, and periodically scries the surrounding wilds. Its tower sight helps chart dangerous ground without waiting for a hero to walk there.

**Ranger Hut** — attracts rangers, reveals ground around it, and calms predator pressure near the settled fields. It can be built on forest or marsh, like the grove. Rangers themselves favour exploration, hunting, and protecting the wild.

**Mead Hall** — brews morale and attracts barbarians. Strong drink and loud boasts make heroes more willing to chase bounty flags.

**Herbalist Grove** — grows herbs, attracts druids, and can lift a hag's curse from a nearby farm. It is also a quiet resting hearth for weary heroes.

**Graveyard** — stores the dead and draws necromancers. Its corpse stock can become a necromancer's servants or, if left unwatched, hostile undead. The burial ground gives **+2 safe population** for plague checks. A shrine's ward separately suppresses hostile risings.

**Sewers** — attract rogues, control plague pressure, and draw rats. They are a sanitation building with a vermin problem attached.

## Heroes and Indirect Control

Heroes arrive because a hall attracts their class. Attraction is periodic and depends on the completed halls you have built. A new hero steps out from an appropriate hall and makes it their home.

Heroes are autonomous. They score the world against their personality and current needs, then choose a goal. A hero may explore, chase a bounty, hunt, loot, shop, rest, carouse, pray, assist with work, or fight something nearby. Their personality weights make some choices much more likely, but they are not puppets.

You influence heroes through:

- **Halls:** the building mix decides which classes arrive.
- **Bounties:** posted rewards make lairs tempting.
- **Safety and comfort:** taverns and groves give them somewhere to recover.
- **The forge:** gear makes them stronger, and they buy it with their own loot.
- **The roster and inspector:** watch what they are doing and dismiss a hero if you need the room.

### Hero Classes

- **Rogue** — attracted by taverns and sewers. A gambler and treasure-chaser who loves bounties, hunts, and carouses.
- **Warrior** — attracted by guardhouses and blacksmiths. Reliable fighter, likes bounties, and will lend a hand at a worksite.
- **Paladin** — attracted by shrines. Drawn to smiting evil, prays at shrines, and is a strong front-line defender.
- **Druid** — attracted by herbalist groves. Protects wild lairs, dislikes pollution, and may leave a realm that defiles too much land.
- **Dwarf Craftsman** — attracted by blacksmiths. Favours assisting the realm and defending it; a steady, practical hero.
- **Wizard** — attracted by wizard towers. Smites evil and explores; wizards do not buy forged gear.
- **Ranger** — attracted by ranger huts. Explores, hunts, and protects the wild. The hut's calm keeps predators off nearby farms.
- **Barbarian** — attracted by mead halls. Loves bounties and carousing; mead morale makes bounty flags even more tempting.
- **Necromancer** — attracted by graveyards. Smites evil in its own way and turns stored corpses into temporary undead servants.

### Hero Levels and Gear

Heroes earn experience from meaningful ventures: kills and den clears, exploring, hunting, tending wild lairs, looting treasure, honest worksite labour, and carousing. A class's primary pursuit pays in full, while activities outside it teach only a small fraction, so heroes grow along the path they already favour rather than by dabbling everywhere. Combat experience is unaffected by this. Each new level asks for a little more experience than the last, with the cost rising gradually faster at higher levels.

Hero attacks are not guaranteed. A **level-1 hero has about 40% accuracy**; each level adds **5 points of accuracy**, up to **90%**. The damage dealt by a hit rises much more slowly than raw level—roughly **+1 every third level**—and den sieges use the same accuracy and damage. Higher levels therefore make heroes more reliable rather than multiplying their damage.

When a hero levels up:

- Maximum health increases, and the hero heals to full.
- Accuracy improves, so veteran heroes miss less often.
- Damage per hit rises only every few levels, so leveling is a steady gain rather than an explosion of power.

**Gear** is the other half of hero power. A blacksmith forges gear points; a hero with enough coin buys one, up to a limit. Each point adds melee strength. The coin goes back into the realm treasury. Wizards do not shop the forge.

Base hero health is deliberately lean, so even a veteran cannot stand in front of a whole warband forever. Higher-level, well-equipped heroes survive ambushes and bring down lairs that would stop a fresh adventurer, but they remain mortal: a hero at low health will try to break off and recover, and a bad fight can kill them. A fallen hero drops their purse as loot for someone else to find.

### Hero Cap, Warnings, and Leaving

The hero cap scales with the living peasant population: roughly one hero for every five people, with a minimum of one. The header shows the current count. Build up the population if you want a larger band.

If the population falls and the realm is over its cap, the realm warns you. After the warnings, some heroes may choose to leave on their own. You can also dismiss a hero directly from their inspector; they walk to the map edge and are gone. Dismissal is useful when you want to make room for a different class or a better arrival.

### Bounties

You can post a bounty on any lair you have charted, including wild lairs as well as hostile camps. You can also post an **explore bounty on a reachable spot of uncharted map**; the first hero to reach that spot charts it and claims the purse. The crown keeps a small fee; the rest goes onto the flag. Bounties stack, so adding more gold makes the flag more tempting.

Heroes decide for themselves whether a bounty is worth the trip. A bounty's pull grows with the reward, but it is strongest on heroes who already care about hunting bounties: rogues are the readiest to chase a flag, barbarians and warriors are close behind, and the less inclined may need a richer reward to stir. The pull is also weighed against the trip and the fight — a far lair or a tough den needs a richer reward to draw the same attention. A rogue may still race toward treasure; a paladin may prefer a nearby evil lair; a barbarian may be pulled by a strong flag and a full mead hall. The same is true of an explore flag: it makes a blank patch of map a destination, but does not command a specific hero to go. This is the main way to aim heroes without giving them orders.

## Combat and Threats

Combat is adjacency-based and resolved as attrition. Guards, heroes, and hostile raiders hit each other when they are next to one another. Defenders can break off when badly hurt; rank-and-file raiders are more likely to flee at the last moment. The dead may leave loot, and heroes gain experience from victories.

**Goblins** live in dens near the wild borders. Hungry dens send warbands at farms and houses. Successful raids make a den bolder and larger. Guards intercept near the realm; heroes are the ones who march out and siege the den itself.

**Bandits** camp near the roads. They muster riders, shadow laden collectors and heroes, and steal what they can carry. A killed bandit may spill the takings onto the road as loot. Tribute can buy a camp's silence.

**Hags** curse farms from the woods. A cursed farm turns away workers and produces nothing until the curse expires, or until a herbalist grove lifts it.

**Rats** come up from sewers and spoil provisions. They are weak individually but can wear down the larder.

**Hostile undead** rise from graveyards that are not warded. They hunt the living, spoil farms, and break into houses. Guards and heroes can put them down.

**Wild creatures** are part of the ecology. Some are prey, some are predators, and some are dangerous only when their territory or food is threatened. You can post a bounty on a wild lair if you want heroes to clear it out.

**Dens and sieges:** hostile lairs can be cleared by heroes or other armed folk standing adjacent and pressing the siege. A cleared den may leave loot and a standing bounty payout. Goblin dens can regrow after a rest, so clearing the same ground twice is normal.

## Ecology and Factions

The wilds run their own food chains. Rabbit warrens feed wolves; spiders raid goblin stores; frogs feed owlbears. Dens have a larder, a habitat, and a set of needs.

- If a lair's larder runs empty for long enough, it migrates toward better ground.
- If its habitat is destroyed or there is nowhere suitable left, it may starve out and disappear.
- Predators with no prey may fall back to raiding farm stores.
- Felling woods or draining marsh can drive out the lairs that depended on that land.

Druids care about this balance; rangers calm predator pressure near farms.

### Kobolds and Gnomes

Kobolds are hill thieves. They filch chickens and trinkets, and they hate gnomes. Their burrows and the gnome enclaves compete for the same hill country.

Gnomes are tinkers. While their trade is open, they send a steady trickle of gold into the realm treasury. Their trade does not put a gadget directly into a hero's pack: the coin pays for halls, bounties, forged gear, and tribute, so open gnome trade is one of the quiet ways the realm outfits and steers its adventurers.

Gnome trade stops when the kobold feud is at a boil. The usual cause is overlapping territory: when kobold and gnome claims press against each other, the tension ledger sours. At its worst, the enclave bars its gates and kobold warbands march. You can restore trade by:

- Paying tribute to the gnome enclave, which buys a temporary truce and cools the feud.
- Clearing one side of the feud.
- Letting one side's lair starve out of the hills.

Kobolds also steal from nearby farms when no enclave is within reach. A kobold burrow that is left alone is a slow drain on the larder.

### Bandits and Tribute

Bandit camps muster riders and rob the roads. Tribute buys a temporary truce: paid bandits stop mustering and release any mugging they were about to commit. When the tribute expires, the camp goes back to its old habits.

Tribute is an inspector action on bandit camps and gnome enclaves. It costs gold, lasts for a limited time, and cannot be paid again while the current tribute still holds. It is a way to buy breathing room, not a permanent alliance.

### Tension and Claims

Factions have a tension ledger. Events shift it, and it slowly drifts back toward neutral. Territory is the biggest source of friction: dens whose claims overlap sour relations. The kobold–gnome feud is the clearest example.

Tribute and lair-clearing are the main player tools for cooling a feud. Clearing a lair removes its claim and can thaw relations immediately; a lair that starves out can do the same. A feud at its worst closes trade and sends warbands; a feud cooled to neutral reopens trade and stops the strikes.

## Graveyards and the Necromancer

A **graveyard** is corpse storage and a source of danger. Deaths near it are buried there, and the graveyard slowly gathers its own stock. Its inspector shows how many corpses it holds.

A stocked graveyard without protection periodically raises a **hostile undead** raider. That undead hunts living folk, spoils farms, and breaks into houses. The risk is suppressed by a **shrine's ward** within range. A warded graveyard still stores corpses, but it does not spawn hostile undead. Whether warded or not, its buried dead ease plague pressure by adding **2 safe population**. That gives you a reason to build one even before a necromancer arrives.

If the realm has a **necromancer**, the graveyard's corpse stock becomes an asset instead. The necromancer spends corpses to raise **allied undead** servants. These servants fight for the realm, can join sieges, and are capped in number. Their loyalty decays over time; when it runs out, they crumble back to dust without leaving a corpse. A shrine's ward does not stop the necromancer from raising these servants; it only keeps the unquiet from rising on their own.

A graveyard is therefore a trade: corpse storage, necromancer power, and plague relief against the risk of hostile dead. Place it thoughtfully, keep a shrine near it if you want to suppress risings, and remember that demolition removes its undead servants with it.

## Sewers, Rats, and Plague

A **sewer** is a small sanitation building with three effects.

First, it attracts rogues as a home hall. Second, it draws rats from the filth. Rats spoil stored provisions, so a sewer can quietly cost you food even though it protects the settlement in other ways. Third, each sewer raises the population the realm can safely support before fever becomes a risk.

Crowded houses without enough sanitation can produce a **plague outbreak**, killing a person and setting back the larder. The risk only begins once the population outgrows its safe capacity: **6 people**, plus **4 more per sewer**, plus **2 per graveyard**. Every **60 ticks**, an overcrowded realm has a **25%** base outbreak chance, rising by **5% for each extra person** beyond that capacity. Guards and heroes can kill rats before they do too much damage, but the drains will keep producing them.

## Failure and Recovery

Realm Builder does not end in a hard loss. Raids can raze buildings, curses can choke farms, plagues and shortages can reduce the population, and heroes can die or leave. The realm can always rebuild, and the Palace's household trickle is there to help a broke realm get back on its feet.

The real cost of failure is time: a razed farm means a hungry season, a lost collector means uncollected taxes, and a dead hero means a new arrival must earn their levels again.

## Quick Tips

- Build a farm and a house before adding expensive halls. The economy comes first.
- Leave gaps between buildings; the placement ghost will tell you when the breathing room is wrong.
- Watch the tax collector's route. Long, unsafe rounds mean uncollected gold.
- Post bounties on the lairs that are actually hurting you, not just the nearest ones.
- Use tribute to buy time when a bandit camp or the kobold–gnome feud is overwhelming you.
- Keep graveyards near a shrine unless you have a necromancer and are ready for the consequences.
- Build sewers before the population becomes crowded, and expect rats once you do. Graveyards also add plague headroom; shrine wards only suppress their risings.
- Heroes reveal the map. A hero with nothing urgent to do is still useful for charting the wilds.
- The hero cap follows population. If heroes keep leaving, the realm may simply be too small for them.
- Better gear and higher levels matter more than more heroes in a hard fight.
